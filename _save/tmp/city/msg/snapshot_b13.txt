CHANGELOG (player-facing)
Batch 6:
- Keys motels: two in five now have a tin hip roof instead of the gable. Their gallery rails are painted white or, on just over half, turquoise, sea green, coral or butter yellow, and the gallery posts are white. Before, every Keys motel in Key Solano had the same gable and brown rails.
- Painted porch trim: on town houses (bungalows, conch houses, folk Victorians, two-storey houses), the porch posts, rails and brackets, the upper gallery's posts and rails, the ceiling under the gallery roof and the gable trusses take the house's trim colour. Before, they showed the brown wood texture under a white tint. The deck rails of the raised Keys houses are painted too. Farmhouses and fishing shacks keep bare wood.
- Eyebrow houses: on most two-storey conch houses without a double gallery, the front eave comes down over the upper windows (Key West's eyebrow house), in tin over a painted soffit.
- Side-gabled ranches and block houses: a third of the ranch houses and a fifth of the block houses have a side-gable roof instead of the hip, so a Westbrook street is no longer one run of hip roofs. The gable ends are a lighter shade of the wall colour.
- Flat-roofed Mediterranean Revival houses: a quarter of the Mediterranean houses (not the ones with a corner tower) have a flat roof behind a stucco parapet, with a barrel-tile pent along the street front, as in Coral Gables and Miami Shores.
Batch 8:
- Vacant lots and street parking. The bare ground left along the streets of the towns (Okahatchee, Harlow, Fort Castell, Key Solano, Ten Palms) and the older city (Calle Luna, North Porto Sol, the Flats, Midtown) is filled in by district:
  - vacant lots of rough grass, green and dry straw, with patches of bare earth (sand in the Keys). Some have the slab and driveway apron of a house or shop long gone, a realtor's FOR SALE board near the sidewalk, a tree or two and scrub;
  - small street parking lots: painted stalls, a lamp, a low wall along the sidewalk with the entrance at one end, and a P sign (PARKING, CUSTOMER PARKING, PUBLIC PARKING, PARKING $5);
  - side yards in the towns: lawn, a board fence or block wall at the back and one side, trees;
  - on the industrial streets of the Flats and Fort Castell: paved yards, some behind a block wall.
  Before, a small-town block often had three or four houses on a side with open ground between them, and from the air the blocks read as empty.
- Mobile homes in Ten Palms, the Keys, Harlow, Okahatchee and Fort Castell and on the farms. A single-wide or double-wide is a long low box on a vinyl-lattice or block skirt. It has lap siding in white, cream or a pale colour, small windows (half of them under aluminium awnings) and, on the older ones, a painted band. The roof is a low tin gable or a shingled roof-over. A wooden deck with steps and a rail stands at the door, some under an aluminium awning, and most in town have a carport. Lots are open, or have a post-and-rail or metal fence.
- Temple-front conch houses: some conch houses in Key Solano, Ten Palms and the Keys have a gable-fronted bay forward of the front. The porch (or the two-level gallery) stands on the longer set-back front beside it, where the door is.
- Keys motels with a lower office end: some of the Keys motels are two buildings, the two-storey room block with its gallery and a one-storey office end with its own roof.
- L-shaped conch houses: the porch, or the two-level gallery, stands in the inner corner beside the front leg and reaches out to the leg's face, with the door under it. Before, it ran across the whole front at the leg's face and floated in front of the set-back part, with the roof behind showing through the gap.
- Gallery ceilings: under the upper deck of a conch house's two-level gallery, the lower porch's ceiling is painted - white trim, or in the Keys on half of them the pale haint blue. Before, it was bare brown boards.
Batch 9:
- Bungalows now come in several types instead of one gabled box:
  - Craftsman side-gabled bungalows: the roof runs on down over a porch across the whole front, with a shed or gable dormer on top.
  - Hipped bungalows with a small hipped dormer.
  - "Airplane" bungalows with a small windowed room sitting astride the ridge.
  - In Calle Luna, North City, the Flats and Midtown (and a few in Westbrook and the Grove): stucco Mission bungalows with a flat roof, a curved gable over the front and a stucco loggia porch.
- American Foursquares in Okahatchee, Harlow, Fort Castell and the farm towns, with some in the city: square two-storey houses with a pyramid roof, a hipped dormer and a porch across the front.
- Craftsman porches: square columns on brick or stone piers with knee walls between them. Some foursquares have round columns instead.
- Earth-tone board siding on the Craftsman houses in the towns (sage, olive, brown, barn red, mustard, slate) with cream trim. Foursquares come in brick, board or stucco.
- Bungalow porches now stand over the front door, and the rail is open at the door and at the spot where a resident stands.
- Flat-roofed Mediterranean houses: their parapet now has an outer face. Before, it showed as a thin edge.
Roads:
- The Grove's east-west streets get proper suffixes (Street, Avenue, Drive, Way, Road, Terrace, Court, Place, Lane, Boulevard). Most of them used to borrow a word from the street-name list instead, e.g. "Coral Gulfstream".
