- Two new house types. Fifties Florida block houses (CBS): pastel stucco, a low hip roof in white, tile or white metal, aluminium window awnings (plain or striped) on most, and a carport. They stand alongside the bungalows of North Porto Sol, Calle Luna, the Flats and Midtown, and in Westbrook, the small towns and the Keys. Folk Victorians: two-storey frame houses with a cross gable, a porch with brackets in the inner corner, a king-post truss and an attic window in the front gable, and a two-strip driveway instead of a garage. They are in Okahatchee, Harlow, Fort Castell and Key Solano, and among the farmhouses.
- Carports replace the garage wing on block houses and MiMo houses: a flat roof on steel posts with a utility room at the back. Half of the MiMo carports also get a screen of fins.
- More house shapes: ranch and block houses with a gable-fronted bay forward of the front (the entry beside it), L-shaped bungalows with the porch in the corner, and fewer plain villas on Key Coral and the bay islands.
- Tile roofs come in three blends (terracotta, brown, aged dark red), so the suburbs no longer read as one orange sheet from the air.
- Yard trees are placed in the front and back yards measured from the house. They no longer stand inside houses, porches, decks, stairs, pools, garages, carports, driveways or back-yard buildings: a blocked tree moves to the mirrored spot or the other yard. Before, 5,357 of 47,580 yard trees stood inside a building.
- Strip-mall and gas-station forecourts follow the ground instead of a slab floating 0.35 m above it (cars and shoppers sank into it). There is a 1 m walk with a curb along the shopfronts, walkable up to 0.5 m. Canopy supports, gas columns and pumps stand on the asphalt. Where the building is lifted to an embankment road's level, the forecourt is a solid plateau walled down to the ground (10 of 834).
